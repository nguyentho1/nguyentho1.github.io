Sep 2026 AI in Healthcare
Why Transformer Models Are Changing How We Think About Tabular Clinical Data

For decades, gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, LightGBM) dominated tabular prediction tasks. The assumption was that attention mechanisms — so powerful in NLP — couldn't capture the row-column structure of clinical spreadsheets. The FT-Transformer paper by Gorishniy et al. (2021) challenged that view, and our own work on PIMA diabetes data confirms it: with proper preprocessing (KNN imputation + ICA), FT-Transformer achieves state-of-the-art performance and remains interpretable via SHAP.

What makes this practically useful in health research? Unlike black-box ensembles, token-based attention lets clinicians see which feature combinations drive individual predictions. For risk stratification in resource-constrained settings like Vietnam, that transparency isn't optional — it's necessary for clinical adoption.

Mar 2026 AI in Healthcare
Network Analysis of Mental Health Symptoms: Moving Beyond the Total Score

The total score approach to mental health screening (e.g., PHQ-9 total ≥ 10 = depression) has been the clinical standard for decades. Network psychopathology offers a complementary lens: symptoms are nodes in a network, and their co-activation patterns — not just their sum — determine clinical course and treatment targets.

In our Mekong Delta study (N = 366, DASS-21), we found that concentration difficulty and hopelessness were the most central nodes in the depression subnetwork — not mood or sleep, which are typically screened first. EBIC-GLASSO regularization with bootstrapped stability checks (CS-coefficient > 0.75) ensured these findings are robust. Manuscript currently under review in Current Psychology (Q1).

Statistical Analysis

An overview of the quantitative methods I apply across my research programs — from classical epidemiological models to deep learning for health data. Primary environments: R (epidemiology & biostatistics) and Python (ML pipelines). Select a technique to see what I did.

Network psychopathology models symptoms as nodes in a graph, estimating partial correlations between them with regularized graphical models (EBIC-GLASSO). Rather than summing symptoms into a total score, networks reveal which symptoms are most central (i.e., most connected), which act as bridges between subnetworks, and how stable these patterns are across bootstrap samples. This approach is particularly informative for comorbid disorders such as depression co-occurring with anxiety and stress.
What I did
  • Estimated EBIC-GLASSO regularized networks for DASS-21 items (N = 366, Mekong Delta high school students), identifying depression, anxiety, and stress symptom clusters.
  • Computed node centrality metrics (strength, betweenness, closeness) and bridge centrality to identify symptoms linking depression and anxiety subnetworks.
  • Performed case-dropping bootstrap stability analysis (CS-coefficient > 0.75) to confirm the robustness of centrality estimates.
  • Visualized networks using spring layout with edge weights; compared network structure between gender subgroups using Network Comparison Test (NCT).
  • Manuscript under review in Current Psychology (Q1) — prevalence and network analysis of depression, anxiety, and stress in Vietnamese high school adolescents.

For tabular clinical data, I work primarily with the FT-Transformer (Feature Tokenizer + Transformer) architecture, which applies attention mechanisms to numerical and categorical features encoded as learned token embeddings. The pipeline includes preprocessing (KNN imputation for missing values, Independent Component Analysis for feature decorrelation), standard cross-validation, hyperparameter tuning, and post-hoc interpretability using SHAP values.
What I did
  • Developed and trained an FT-Transformer model on the PIMA Indian Diabetes dataset (N = 768 women, 8 clinical features) for high-risk diabetes classification.
  • Applied KNN imputation to handle physiologically implausible zero values (glucose, BMI, insulin), then Independent Component Analysis (ICA) to decorrelate features before tokenization.
  • Achieved state-of-the-art AUC on PIMA (AUC = 0.88, F1 = 0.82) with 5-fold stratified cross-validation and grid search over learning rate, depth, and attention heads.
  • Generated SHAP feature importance plots and individual-level waterfall explanations for clinical interpretability.
  • Published first-author in Springer LNICST (RAIDS 2024); extended clinical decision system published at ICASI 2025 (IET).

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) extends regression by simultaneously estimating multiple equations, allowing latent variable measurement, direct and indirect effects, and model fit evaluation. For my child maltreatment and depression research, SEM made it possible to test theoretically derived causal pathways — such as the mediation of household dysfunction on depression through cumulative maltreatment — in a single coherent model.
What I did
  • Specified and estimated CFA measurement models for maltreatment subtypes (physical, emotional, neglect) and depression (PHQ-A items) in lavaan.
  • Tested mediation pathways: household dysfunction → cumulative maltreatment → depressive symptoms, with bootstrapped indirect effects (B = 5,000) for confidence intervals.
  • Evaluated model fit using RMSEA (< 0.06), CFI (> 0.95), SRMR (< 0.08); performed sensitivity analysis with alternative path structures.
  • Used path analysis (observed variables only) for smaller sub-samples where full latent SEM was not identified.
  • Applied SEM for MPH thesis (N = 1,200, 8 schools, Bac Giang Province); partial invariance tested across gender groups using configural and metric models.

Logistic regression remains the workhorse of epidemiological analysis for binary outcomes: odds ratios, confidence intervals, and model diagnostics are well understood by clinical audiences. For ordered outcomes (severity scales, educational grades, frequency categories), the proportional odds model (ordinal logistic regression) preserves the ordinality of the outcome without assuming linear spacing between levels. I also apply Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA) — a deterministic complement to regression that identifies bottleneck predictors.
What I did
  • Ran multivariate logistic regression models for binary maltreatment outcomes (physical abuse: yes/no) controlling for age, sex, school, and household-level covariates; applied stepwise selection with AIC and manual confounder adjustment following DAG guidance.
  • Fitted ordinal logistic regression models (polr in R) for injury severity outcomes and depression severity categories; tested and reported the proportional odds assumption (Brant test).
  • Performed NCA to identify necessary conditions for child maltreatment — predictors without which maltreatment could not occur regardless of other factors. Reported effect size d (ceiling envelope area) with permutation-based significance.
  • Published results in Vietnam Journal of Preventive Medicine (Vol. 34, No. 06) and Child Abuse & Neglect (Q1, under review).

Causal inference methods allow researchers to move beyond association toward estimation of causal effects in observational data — where randomization is not possible. The core toolkit includes directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) for structural confounding identification, followed by estimation via inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) or propensity-score matching. Sensitivity analyses (E-values, tipping-point analysis) assess robustness to unmeasured confounding.
What I did
  • Constructed causal DAGs for the relationship between household dysfunction and child maltreatment, identifying minimal sufficient adjustment sets using dagitty (web tool) and the dagR package.
  • Estimated average treatment effects using IPTW (WeightIt + sandwich variance estimators) for the effect of household dysfunction on cumulative maltreatment in the SNSF study sample (N = 1,200).
  • Performed overlap diagnostics (propensity score histogram, effective sample size), love plots for covariate balance, and tipping-point E-value analysis for unmeasured confounding.
  • Applied in manuscript currently under review in Child Abuse & Neglect (Q1) — household dysfunction and academic pressure as correlates of cumulative maltreatment among Vietnamese adolescents.

Clinical prediction models estimate individual-level risk of an outcome — disease onset, treatment response, adverse event — from a set of predictor variables. TRIPOD guidelines define reporting standards: discrimination (AUC/C-statistic), calibration (calibration curve, Hosmer-Lemeshow), and internal validation (cross-validation or bootstrapping). When the dataset is small, shrinkage methods (ridge, lasso, elastic net) reduce overfitting.
What I did
  • Developed and internally validated a logistic regression-based clinical prediction model for depression risk in Vietnamese secondary school adolescents; predictors selected by LASSO with 10-fold cross-validation.
  • Reported model calibration using a calibration curve (loess smooth) and Hosmer-Lemeshow test; discrimination reported as AUC with 95% CI via DeLong method (pROC package).
  • Applied to the FT-Transformer diabetes study: compared model calibration across logistic regression, XGBoost, and FT-Transformer; FT-Transformer showed superior calibration and AUC.
  • Currently applying TRIPOD-AI reporting standards for the clinical decision support system paper (ICASI 2025, IET).

Common pitfalls
  • Spaces in column names — use depression_score, not depression score. Spaces can break the column header parsing.
  • Missing values must be truly empty — do not write NA, N/A, -, ., or 999 as missing codes. Leave the cell blank in Excel before saving as CSV.
  • Ordinal categories must sort in the right order — always use numbers (1, 2, 3, 4) for DASS-21 severity levels. Text labels sort alphabetically, which may not match clinical order.
  • Mixed text and numbers in one column — if a column has even one text entry (e.g. an accidental comma), the whole column may be treated as categorical and excluded from regression.
  • Too few cases per group — Binary LR needs at least 10 events per predictor (EPP rule). With 3 predictors you need ≥30 cases in the smaller outcome group.
  • DASS-21 tip — for multi-outcome analyses, create separate columns for each subscale total (depression_total, anxiety_total, stress_total) and check all three in the Outcome Variables list.
